The 30-second version

  • On Tuesday, voters decide which party controls Congress for the last two years of President Trump’s term. Congress has two halves: the House and the Senate.
  • The party that wins a half runs it. They decide which bills get voted on, who leads the committees, and who gets investigated.
  • Each half needs a majority to be in control. In the House that’s 218 seats. In the Senate it’s 51 for Democrats (the reason is below).
  • This site lets you track the results live. When a race gets called on TV, click the winner and watch the scoreboard move.

What’s on the ballot

A “midterm” happens halfway through a president’s four-year term. It’s basically a mid-term report card, and the president’s party usually loses ground.

The 35 Senate seats are 33 regular elections plus 2 special elections (Ohio and Florida) for seats whose holders left mid-term. The other 65 senators aren’t up until 2028 or 2030, which is why the Senate tab starts the scoreboard at 34 Democrats and 31 Republicans.

Why 51 for Democrats — and why people say 52

This is the question everyone asks, and it’s the reason the Senate tab has two dashed lines.

  1. The Senate has 100 members, so a majority is 51.
  2. A 50–50 tie is broken by the Vice President, who presides over the Senate. Right now that’s JD Vance, a Republican. So Republicans keep control with just 50 seats.
  3. That means Democrats need 51 outright. That’s the main finish line on the Senate tab: 51 · D outright
  4. Here’s the catch: John Fetterman. He’s a Democratic senator from Pennsylvania, he isn’t up for election until 2028, and his seat is one of the 34 Democratic seats that aren’t on the ballot this year. He has broken with his party on several big votes, spoke at the Republican convention in September, and Trump has publicly urged him to switch parties. He hasn’t said he will; he told CBS he’d revisit his plans after the election.
  5. Why that matters: if Democrats win exactly 51 and Fetterman switched, it would be 50–50 with Vance breaking ties, and Republicans would be back in charge. So some analysts say Democrats really want 52, a majority that survives losing him. That’s the second line: 52 · D w/o Fetterman

Bottom line: 51 is the official line, and 52 is the safety cushion.

How to use the trackers

  1. Pick a tab at the top: Senate or House.
  2. Wait for a race to be called. News outlets like AP, CNN and Fox “call” a race when they’re confident who won.
  3. Click the winner’s name on that race’s card. It lights up and the scoreboard and bar update. Click again to undo.
  4. Watch the dashed lines. The first party to cross its line wins control. “Reset all calls” clears everything.

This isn’t a live feed. It only changes when you click. Your clicks are saved in your own browser and nobody else sees them.

What it means for Trump

Trump stays president no matter what happens Tuesday. The question is how much Congress helps him or holds him back for his last two years. Here’s what changes in each scenario.

Republicans keep both

HOUSE RSENATE R
  • Congress stays lined up with the White House.
  • Trump’s priorities can pass the House easily. In the Senate, budget bills need a simple majority and most other bills need 60 votes.
  • His nominees (cabinet, judges) keep getting confirmed.
  • Few congressional investigations of the administration.

Democrats win the House only

HOUSE DSENATE R
  • Democrats pick the Speaker and chair every House committee.
  • They can investigate: hold hearings and subpoena documents and officials.
  • Any bill Trump wants has to get through a Democratic-run House, and government spending bills need Democratic votes.
  • They could vote to impeach, but removing a president takes two-thirds of the Senate (67 votes).

Democrats win the Senate only

HOUSE RSENATE D
  • The Senate approves judges, cabinet members and Supreme Court justices. Democrats could block them.
  • Democrats would run Senate committees and decide what gets a vote.
  • Bills passed by the Republican House could stall in the Senate.
  • A 51-seat majority is thin. This is where the Fetterman question matters most.

Democrats win both

HOUSE DSENATE D
  • Congress becomes a major check on Trump: investigations from the House, blocked nominees from the Senate.
  • Very little of his legislative agenda could pass.
  • Budget and shutdown fights get bigger, since funding bills need both chambers.

What doesn’t change in any scenario:

  • Trump keeps his veto. Congress needs two-thirds of both chambers to override it.
  • He can still sign executive orders and direct federal agencies, though courts can review them.
  • Congress’s power is mostly about bills, money, confirmations and oversight. It can’t simply undo what the president does on his own.

Election night survival tips

  1. Don’t expect an answer at 8 PM. Control of Congress usually comes down to a handful of close races, and those are the slowest to call.
  2. Early numbers can mislead. Some states count in-person votes first and mail ballots later (or the reverse), so a lead can shrink or grow as counting finishes.
  3. The West Coast reports last. California and other late-closing states can take days to finish, so the final answer may not be clear on Tuesday night.

Quick glossary

Incumbent
The person who currently holds the seat and is running to keep it.
Open seat
A seat with no incumbent running, because they retired, lost a primary, or are running for something else.
Flip
When a seat changes parties. Democrats need to flip Republican seats to take over, and vice versa.
Call (or “projection”)
When news outlets announce a winner before every vote is counted.
Caucus with
An independent senator who votes with one party to organize the Senate. On the Senate tab, Nebraska and South Dakota independents are marked this way.
Speaker of the House
The leader of the party that controls the House. Decides which bills get a vote.
Filibuster
A Senate rule that means most bills need 60 votes to move forward, not just 51.
Subpoena
A legal order to hand over documents or testify. Committee chairs in the majority party control these.
Confirmation
The Senate vote to approve a president’s picks for cabinet, judges and other top jobs.
Cook Political Report
A nonpartisan newsletter that rates how competitive every race is. The House tab uses its ratings.
